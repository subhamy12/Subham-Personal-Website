Inflation Reduction Act (2022)

A big US law that pays people and companies to use clean energy, lets Medicare bargain on some drug prices, and adds a minimum tax for very large corporations.

Reading level

The government offered money back on taxes if you buy things like electric cars, heat pumps or solar panels, and gave companies credits to build clean energy. Medicare got the power to negotiate prices for some expensive drugs, and seniors' insulin costs were capped at $35 a month. To help pay for it, giant companies (over $1 billion in profit) must pay at least 15% tax, and the IRS got more funding to collect taxes owed.

What changes

  • Tax credits for EVs, heat pumps, rooftop solar and home efficiency upgrades
  • Production and investment credits for wind, solar, batteries and domestic manufacturing
  • Medicare can negotiate prices for a set of high-cost drugs
  • $35/month insulin cap for Medicare enrollees
  • 15% minimum tax for corporations with over $1B in profits; 1% tax on stock buybacks

Each side is written the way its own supporters would make the case. Neither is endorsed. The middle column covers what is actually contested.

Case from the left

Biggest US climate investment ever, plus relief on drug prices.

  • Speeds up the shift to clean energy and cuts emissions.
  • Medicare negotiation lowers costs for seniors and taxpayers.
  • Asks very large, profitable companies to pay a minimum share.
Neutral read

Most economists agree the law's direct effect on inflation was small either way; its main effects are on energy investment and drug pricing. The final cost depends heavily on how many people and firms claim the uncapped credits.

Open questions
  • How much did the credits actually cost vs. projections?
  • Did drug negotiation affect pharmaceutical R&D?
Case from the right

Costly subsidies that pick winners and didn't reduce inflation.

  • Credits cost far more than originally estimated as uptake grew.
  • Drug price controls could reduce investment in new medicines.
  • Extra IRS funding raises concerns about audits on ordinary taxpayers.
