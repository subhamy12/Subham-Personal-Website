France: Raising the Retirement Age to 64

France gradually raised the minimum retirement age from 62 to 64 to keep the pension system funded, pushing it through without a final vote in the National Assembly.

Reading level

French workers could retire at 62. This reform slowly raises that to 64 and makes people work more years to get a full pension. The government says the system would run out of money otherwise. Millions protested, and the government used a special constitutional power to pass it without a final vote in parliament.

What changes

  • Minimum retirement age rises from 62 to 64
  • 43 years of contributions needed for a full pension, sooner
  • Exceptions for people who began working very young

Each side is written the way its own supporters would make the case. Neither is endorsed. The middle column covers what is actually contested.

Case from the left

Unfair and undemocratic: workers pay, and parliament never voted.

  • Hits manual workers with shorter lives hardest.
  • Could have been funded by taxing wealth or businesses instead.
Neutral read

Economists agree the pension system faces deficits, but disagree on how large and whether raising the age is the best fix. The use of Article 49.3 became as controversial as the policy itself.

Open questions
  • Will a future government pause or reverse the reform?
Case from the right

Unpopular but necessary as people live longer.

  • Fewer workers are supporting each retiree.
  • Most European countries already retire later than 64.
